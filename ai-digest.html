How to Use NVIDIA Warp and MjWarp to Accelerate Robotics Simulation and Learning Workflows

Hugging Face Blog
  • Warp is NVIDIA's Python framework for authoring GPU kernels that compile to CUDA; MJWarp reimplements MuJoCo's physics pipeline on top of it for batched simulation.
  • Solves a real RL bottleneck: classic MuJoCo is fast per-robot on CPU but doesn't parallelize, while MJWarp runs thousands of independent environments at once on a GPU using the same MJCF robot models.
  • Walks through validating GPU/CPU parity, sizing contact and constraint buffers, and using CUDA graph capture, scaling an SO-101 arm task from one CPU world to 2,048 parallel GPU worlds.

Research & Papers

COMED: The Missing Middle Between Routing and Collaboration in Multi-LLM Inference

ArXiv cs.CL
  • Routing picks one model and stops; dense multi-model collaboration consults peers on every query and can just as easily hurt a correct answer as save a wrong one.
  • COMED (Controlled Model Escalation for Multi-LLM Deliberation) escalates selectively instead, using self-consistency checks, routing-confidence margins, and lightweight peer assessment to decide when a second model is worth the cost.
  • Gains up to +10.7 points on MedQA and lifts GPT-4.5 on HLE from 23.1% to 28.1%, beating dense collaboration while using fewer model invocations and decoded tokens.

Experts Rise Where LLMs Disagree: Using Cross-Model Disagreement to Target Expert Effort in LLM Codebook Revision for Large-Scale Annotation

ArXiv cs.CL
  • Targets a real bottleneck: hand-building an annotation codebook for large-scale text labeling normally takes months of expert time.
  • Uses disagreement between multiple LLMs to flag exactly which cases need expert attention, then tests three ways experts can respond: revising LLM output, answering targeted questions, or labeling disputes with a rationale.
  • On tutoring-session annotation, rationale labeling reached 64.9% agreement with expert labels, the best of the three methods, while cutting codebook development from months to days.

Recognized but Not Produced: A Generation Benchmark for Culturally Specific Kinship Terms

ArXiv cs.CL
  • Tests five open-weight LLMs on generating, not just selecting, kinship terms in Hindi, Tamil, and Korean across two conversational scenarios.
  • Finds a large recognition-production gap: GPT-OSS 120B picks the correct term in multiple choice 90.7% of the time but produces an accepted term unprompted only 36% of the time; Llama 3.3 70B drops similarly, 77.9% to 24.2%.
  • Paternal-lineage terms are favored unevenly across languages; the authors argue generation benchmarks are needed alongside multiple-choice ones to actually measure cultural-vocabulary competence.

Classifying Interpretive Canons at the Sentence Level: A Benchmark from the German Federal Constitutional Court

ArXiv cs.CL
  • Builds a sentence-level benchmark applying Larenz's legal-interpretation framework to real opinions from the German Federal Constitutional Court.
  • Evaluates four LLMs with both expert-written and genetically-optimized prompts; F1 lands between 70.4 and 79.2, with grammatical interpretation easiest to identify and systematic interpretation hardest.
  • Genetic prompt optimization didn't reliably beat expert-crafted prompts, a data point against assuming automated prompt search always wins. Accepted at the ICML 2026 AI4Law workshop.

When Learned Context Planning Fails to Beat Strong Retrieval: A Controlled Study of Planning, Routing, and Reranking for Long-Context QA

ArXiv cs.CL
  • Tests whether training a model to pick relevant "evidence atoms" before answering beats well-tuned retrieval on long-context multiple-choice QA (LongBench-v2, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct).
  • At an 18k-character budget, anchored hybrid retrieval (36.18%) and BM25 (35.98%) both beat the best learned planner (34.19%); the gap holds on a held-out 152-question split, 42.11% vs 36.84%.
  • Under tight token budgets the planner is a wash at best, ahead by 0.4 points at 6k and losing by 9k, leading the authors to call learned planning "a weak relevance signal" rather than a retrieval replacement.
