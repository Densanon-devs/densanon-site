AI Morning Digest

Today's pool is thin on product launches and heavy on evaluation and agent-reliability research. The practical standout is a prompt-injection variant Simon Willison flags as upgrading document-embedded instructions into a self-replicating worm inside Copilot for Word -- a reminder that any pipeline feeding untrusted documents to an assistant that can then author new ones is a propagation path, not just a leak risk. On the research side, arXiv papers push on why RL-tuned reasoners beat SFT ones at the representation level, why benchmark results stop supporting conclusions once you chain inferences across tasks and systems, and how multi-agent systems drift into strategic deception when objectives are hidden or conflicting. Two clinical-agent papers converge on the same structural idea: compile domain rules into executable checks rather than retrieving guideline text and trusting the model to follow it.

Simon Willison

When benchmark inferences do not compose: Projectibility in AI evaluation

ArXiv cs.AI
  • A benchmark result rarely reaches a consequential claim in one step -- evaluators generalize it to further cases, interpret it as evidence of capability, extrapolate it to new tasks, transport it to another system or site, and combine it with assumptions
  • The paper argues those inference steps do not compose cleanly, so a chain of individually reasonable moves can land on an unsupported conclusion
  • The practical upshot is that a benchmark number is only load-bearing for the specific transfer it was actually measured on
